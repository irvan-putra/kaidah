# Publishing research and contributions

## Public by default

Research you did yourself, from public sources, is a good candidate to publish. Set that as a standing default so the assistant does not ask each time for clearly generic work. Cite the sources.

Review before publishing:

- Remove personal details, chat context and second-person notes meant only for you.
- Keep implementation plans, backups and personal notes separate from research.
- Check that the public version does not overstate what was confirmed. Keep open questions marked as open.

## When research involves other people

Someone else's input has two separate questions. Ask both, and do not merge them.

1. **Permission to publish.** May the contribution be shared publicly at all?
2. **Attribution.** If yes, do they want their real name, a pseudonym, or no attribution?

Notes:

- Contributions shared for a limited purpose stay limited to that purpose, even if the content is also public elsewhere.
- Anonymous does not mean claiming you found it yourself. You can say "a contributor who prefers not to be named", or leave the origin out if they agree.
- Even an anonymous mention can give too much away. If so, leave the origin out, or write the point fresh from public sources and publish only what those sources support.
- Public evidence can support a new write-up. It does not cancel permission limits on the original contribution. Sometimes the safest choice is to leave the point out.
- Never invent a source or claim original discovery.

## Old research

Audit older work in three groups: safe to publish, needs editing, needs someone else's permission. Verify the actual content and the actual access setting of each item. Do not sort by title.

## House rule: the Chatham House Rule

When a conversation or event is held under the Chatham House Rule, use it as Chatham House defines it. The official wording is:

> When a meeting, or part thereof, is held under the Chatham House Rule, participants are free to use the information received, but neither the identity nor the affiliation of the speaker(s), nor that of any other participant, may be revealed.

Source: https://www.chathamhouse.org/about-us/chatham-house-rule (checked 16 Nov 2026).

What the Rule says, and what it does not:

- When validly in force, the information received may be used. The identity and affiliation of the speakers and of any other participant may not be revealed.
- It does not authorize revealing identities or affiliations, and it does not override separate confidentiality or legal restrictions.
- Hinting at the source can reveal identity. Avoid details that let readers work out who said it.

Recommended practice (our suggestion, not part of the official wording):

- Agree and communicate the scope beforehand, and do not apply the Rule after the fact.
- When writing up, avoid revealing the meeting or who attended if that would itself identify a source.
- If a point cannot be shared without exposing its source, leave it out or restate it from independent public sources.
